Myocardial Infarction — Clinical Deep Dive

Myocardial Infarction is defined as myocardial cell death due to prolonged ischaemia. Clinically, it is diagnosed when there is a rise and/or fall of cardiac troponin (cTn) with at least one value above the 99th percentile upper reference limit (URL) AND at least one of:

  • ① Symptoms of acute myocardial ischaemia
  • ② New ischaemic ECG changes
  • ③ Development of pathological Q waves
  • ④ Imaging evidence of new loss of viable myocardium or new RWMA
  • ⑤ Identification of coronary thrombus by angiography or autopsy

MI Classification Types:

Type 1

Spontaneous MI due to atherosclerotic plaque rupture, erosion, fissuring, or dissection causing intraluminal thrombus → myocardial ischaemia → necrosis. This is the "classic heart attack."

Type 2

MI secondary to oxygen supply-demand mismatch — NOT due to acute coronary plaque disruption. Causes: tachyarrhythmia, hypotension, severe anaemia, coronary spasm, coronary embolism, respiratory failure.

Type 3

MI resulting in death when biomarker values are unavailable. Sudden cardiac death with symptoms suggestive of ischaemia + presumed new ECG changes or VF, but death occurs before blood samples or troponin rise.

Type 4a

MI related to PCI (≤48 hrs). Troponin >5× URL with normal baseline OR >20% rise if elevated baseline + ischaemic symptoms/ECG/angiographic findings/imaging.

Type 4b

MI related to stent thrombosis — documented by angiography or autopsy using Academic Research Consortium criteria.

Type 5

MI related to CABG. Troponin >10× URL within 48 hours + new pathological Q waves OR angiographic graft/native artery occlusion OR new RWMA.

The ischaemic cascade is the sequential pathophysiological response to myocardial oxygen deprivation. From my ICU experience, understanding this cascade is critical for timing interventions:

0–10 seconds — Metabolic Switch

Aerobic metabolism ceases. Shift to anaerobic glycolysis → lactate accumulation → intracellular acidosis. ATP production drops from 36 to 2 molecules per glucose. Creatine phosphate reserves depleted within seconds.

10–30 seconds — Diastolic Dysfunction

ATP depletion impairs calcium reuptake into sarcoplasmic reticulum → diastolic relaxation failure → ↑ LVEDP. This is detectable by Doppler echo (E/A reversal) BEFORE any systolic impairment. Clinically: dyspnoea, pulmonary congestion onset.

Seconds to Minutes — Systolic Dysfunction (RWMA)

Regional wall motion abnormalities appear. Hypokinesis → akinesis → dyskinesis. If >40% of LV wall involved → cardiogenic shock. The LVEF begins to decline. This is the "stunned" myocardium phase — potentially reversible with reperfusion.

Minutes — ECG Changes

ECG changes follow functional impairment. Sequence: Tall hyperacute T waves → ST elevation → Q wave development. In the ER, I've seen hyperacute T waves precede ST elevation by 15–30 minutes — this is the window where early recognition saves lives.

Minutes to Hours — Chest Pain & Autonomic Response

Adenosine, bradykinin, & lactate stimulate cardiac nociceptors (C-fibres via sympathetic afferents → T1–T5 dorsal root ganglia). Referred pain: left arm, jaw, epigastrium. Inferior MI → vagal activation (Bezold-Jarisch reflex) → bradycardia + hypotension. Anterior MI → sympathetic surge → tachycardia + hypertension.

20–40 Minutes — Irreversible Necrosis (Wavefront Phenomenon)

Necrosis begins subendocardially and progresses transmurally over hours (Reimer & Jennings "wavefront of necrosis"). Subendocardium is most vulnerable due to: highest metabolic demand, highest wall tension (Laplace's law), compression during systole, furthest from epicardial coronary supply. This is WHY "time is muscle" — every minute of delay = more transmural extension.

Correlating ECG leads with coronary territory is the single most important skill in acute MI management. From my experience, getting this right in the first 5 minutes dictates the entire treatment pathway:

ECG Leads Territory Artery Clinical Pearl
V1–V4 Anterior LAD Highest mortality. Pump failure, cardiogenic shock. "Widow maker" if proximal LAD.
II, III, aVF Inferior RCA (85%) / LCx (15%) Always check V4R for RV infarct. Avoid nitrates + diuretics if RV involved → volume dependent.
I, aVL, V5–V6 Lateral LCx / Diagonal Often "electrically silent" — LCx occlusion may show only subtle changes. High index of suspicion needed.
V1–V3 (reciprocal ST↓) Posterior LCx / PDA Check V7–V9 (posterior leads). Tall R in V1 + ST depression V1-V3 = posterior MI until proven otherwise.
V3R, V4R Right Ventricle Proximal RCA Hypotension + clear lungs + elevated JVP = RV MI triad. Fluid challenge, NOT diuretics. Avoid morphine & nitrates.

STEMI

  • Pathology: Complete thrombotic coronary occlusion → transmural ischaemia
  • ECG: ST elevation ≥1mm in ≥2 contiguous leads (≥2mm in V1-V3 for men ≥40, ≥2.5mm in V1-V3 for men <40, ≥1.5mm in V1-V3 for women)
  • Troponin: Markedly elevated — but DO NOT wait for troponin to initiate reperfusion
  • Management: Emergent reperfusion — Primary PCI (preferred) within 90 min OR Thrombolysis within 30 min if PCI not available within 120 min
  • STEMI Equivalents: New LBBB (Sgarbossa criteria), de Winter T waves, Wellens syndrome (LAD), posterior MI pattern, isolated ST elevation in aVR (left main / 3-vessel)

NSTEMI / Unstable Angina

  • Pathology: Partial/subtotal occlusion OR complete occlusion with collateral supply → subendocardial ischaemia
  • ECG: ST depression ≥0.5mm, T-wave inversion, or transient ST elevation, or even normal ECG
  • Troponin: Elevated in NSTEMI; normal in Unstable Angina (UA). High-sensitivity troponin (hs-cTn) with serial measurements at 0h/1h or 0h/3h protocol
  • Risk Stratification: GRACE score determines timing of angiography: Very high risk → <2h, High risk → <24h, Intermediate → <72h, Low risk → non-invasive testing first
  • Very High Risk Criteria: Haemodynamic instability, recurrent/ongoing chest pain refractory to medical Rx, life-threatening arrhythmias, mechanical complications, acute HF, recurrent dynamic ST/T changes

⚡ CLINICAL PEARL FROM ICU EXPERIENCE

"In my years in the CCU, I've learned that the first 10 minutes are about RECOGNITION, the next 20 are about STABILIZATION, and everything after is about REPERFUSION. The biggest killer isn't the MI itself — it's delay."

M
Morphine (with caution)

2-4mg IV q5-15min PRN. Reserve for pain refractory to nitrates. ⚠️ AVOID in RV infarct, hypotension, respiratory depression. Modern guidelines de-emphasize morphine — may mask ongoing ischaemia and delay recognition of complications. Fentanyl 25-50mcg IV is an alternative.

O
Oxygen

Only if SpO₂ <90% or respiratory distress. AVOID routine O₂ in normoxic patients — DETO₂X trial showed potential harm with hyperoxia (coronary vasoconstriction, increased ROS). Target SpO₂ 94-98%.

N
Nitrates

GTN 0.4mg SL q5min ×3, then IV infusion (5-200 mcg/min) titrate to pain relief and BP. CONTRAINDICATIONS: SBP <90, HR <50 or >100, RV infarct, PDE5 inhibitor use within 24-48 hrs, severe aortic stenosis. Mechanism: venodilation → ↓ preload → ↓ MVO₂, plus coronary vasodilation.

A
Antiplatelet Therapy (Dual — DAPT)

Aspirin: 325mg chewable (NOT enteric-coated) stat → 81mg daily thereafter. Irreversible COX-1 inhibition → blocks TXA₂ synthesis → ↓ platelet aggregation.
P2Y12 Inhibitor (load ASAP):
• Ticagrelor 180mg load → 90mg BID (preferred per ESC/AHA — faster onset, reversible, superior to Clopidogrel per PLATO trial)
• Prasugrel 60mg load → 10mg daily (contraindicated if prior stroke/TIA, age ≥75, weight <60kg)
• Clopidogrel 600mg load → 75mg daily (if ticagrelor/prasugrel contraindicated or unavailable, or with thrombolysis)

B
Beta-Blockers & Anticoagulation

Beta-Blockers: Metoprolol 5mg IV q5min ×3 (if haemodynamically stable, no HF, no AV block) → then oral 25-50mg q6-12h. ↓ HR, ↓ contractility, ↓ MVO₂, ↓ arrhythmia risk. AVOID if: Killip III-IV, SBP <120, HR <60, PR >0.24s, 2nd/3rd degree AV block, active wheeze.
Anticoagulation: UFH 60 U/kg bolus (max 4000U) → 12 U/kg/hr (max 1000U/hr) for PCI pathway. Enoxaparin 0.5mg/kg IV for thrombolysis pathway.

Cardiogenic Shock (Killip IV)

SBP <90 for >30min, CI <2.2, PCWP >18. Usually >40% LV mass infarcted. Mortality 50-80%. Management: Emergent PCI, IABP/Impella, vasopressors (norepinephrine first-line), dobutamine for inotropy.

Free Wall Rupture

Day 3-5 post-MI (or Day 1 with thrombolysis). Sudden haemodynamic collapse + PEA. Pericardial tamponade → beck's triad. Almost universally fatal without emergent surgery. Risk factors: first MI, anterior, female, age >70.

VSD (Ventricular Septal Rupture)

Day 3-5. New harsh pansystolic murmur + thrill at left sternal border. Acute L→R shunt → biventricular failure. Confirm by echo with colour Doppler + step-up in O₂ sat from RA→RV. Surgical repair; Impella or IABP as bridge.

Acute Mitral Regurgitation

Papillary muscle rupture (posteromedial > anterolateral, because posteromedial has single blood supply from PDA). Acute severe MR → flash pulmonary oedema. New systolic murmur (may be soft due to equalization of pressures). Emergent surgery.

LV Aneurysm

Weeks to months post-MI. Persistent ST elevation on ECG (>6 weeks). Thin dyskinetic wall → thrombus formation risk → systemic embolism. Anticoagulation needed. May cause refractory HF or VT.

Dressler Syndrome

2-10 weeks post-MI. Autoimmune pericarditis: fever, pleuritic chest pain, pericardial friction rub, pericardial effusion. ESR/CRP elevated. Treatment: Aspirin high-dose + Colchicine. Avoid anticoagulation (haemorrhagic pericarditis risk).

Cardiac Arrhythmias — Complete Clinical Reference

Arrhythmias in the context of MI are the leading cause of pre-hospital cardiac death. In the CCU, I've managed everything from benign PVCs to pulseless VT within the same shift. The key clinical principle: treat the patient, not the monitor.

Tachyarrhythmias (HR >100 bpm)

Ventricular Fibrillation

Mechanism: Chaotic, disorganized ventricular electrical activity. Multiple re-entrant wavelets. No coordinated contraction → NO cardiac output → cardiac arrest.

ECG: Bizarre, irregular, no discernible P/QRS/T. "Coarse VF" (higher amplitude, more recently arrested, better prognosis) vs "Fine VF" (low amplitude, may mimic asystole — check gain).

In MI context: Occurs in 5-10% of STEMI, usually within first hour. Primary VF (first 48h) does NOT worsen long-term prognosis if survived. Late VF (>48h) = poor prognosis, consider ICD.

Ventricular Tachycardia

Monomorphic VT: Single re-entrant circuit around infarct scar. Broad complex (QRS >120ms), regular, rate 150-250. Concordance across precordial leads, AV dissociation, capture/fusion beats → confirms VT (Brugada algorithm).

Polymorphic VT: Changing QRS morphology. If QTc normal → ischaemia-driven (treat the ischaemia). If QTc prolonged → Torsades de Pointes (IV Magnesium 2g, overdrive pacing, Isoproterenol).

Clinical pearl: "All wide-complex tachycardia is VT until proven otherwise." — if in doubt, treat as VT. Misdiagnosing VT as SVT with aberrancy and giving Verapamil → haemodynamic collapse & death.

⚡ Management (AHA/ERC Guidelines)
  • Pulseless VT/VF: Immediate CPR + Defibrillation (biphasic 120-200J, mono 360J) → Epinephrine 1mg IV q3-5min → Amiodarone 300mg IV bolus (then 150mg) → Repeat shocks. Address reversible causes (4H's & 4T's).
  • Pulsed VT (stable): Amiodarone 150mg IV over 10min → infusion 1mg/min ×6h → 0.5mg/min ×18h. OR Lidocaine 1-1.5mg/kg bolus → 1-4mg/min infusion. Procainamide 20mg/min up to 17mg/kg.
  • Pulsed VT (unstable — hypotension, altered consciousness, chest pain, HF): Synchronized cardioversion 100-200J. Sedate first if time permits (midazolam/propofol).
Atrial Fibrillation

Mechanism: Multiple re-entrant wavelets in atrial myocardium OR focal triggers (commonly from pulmonary veins).

ECG: Irregularly irregular rhythm, absent P waves, fibrillatory baseline, variable ventricular rate (typically 100-160 if untreated).

In MI: Occurs in 10-20% of acute MI. Signifies extensive atrial ischaemia, ↑ atrial pressure (LV failure), or pericarditis. Worsens prognosis due to loss of atrial kick (↓ CO by ~25%) and ↑ MVO₂ from tachycardia.

Atrial Flutter

Mechanism: Macro re-entrant circuit in right atrium (typical = counterclockwise around tricuspid annulus through cavotricuspid isthmus).

ECG: "Sawtooth" flutter waves best seen in II, III, aVF, V1. Atrial rate ~300bpm with 2:1 block → ventricular rate ~150. If patient has "SVT at 150" → always consider flutter with 2:1 block (carotid sinus massage may unmask flutter waves).

Management: Rate control similar to AF. Cardioversion at lower energy (50-100J). Highly amenable to catheter ablation (CTI ablation, >95% success).

AF Management in Acute MI

Rate control (first-line in MI): Beta-blockers (Metoprolol 5mg IV) if no HF/hypotension. Diltiazem 0.25mg/kg IV if beta-blocker contraindicated (BUT avoid in systolic HF). Digoxin 0.5mg IV if HF present (slow onset — 2h). Amiodarone 300mg IV over 1h for rate + rhythm control in haemodynamically unstable HF.

Anticoagulation: Heparin acutely (already on it for MI). Long-term: CHA₂DS₂-VASc score. Post-MI with AF + PCI = "triple therapy" dilemma (DAPT + OAC) → use shortest duration possible, prefer DOACs over warfarin (PIONEER AF-PCI, RE-DUAL PCI, AUGUSTUS trials).

AVNRT (60-70% of SVTs): Dual AV nodal pathways (fast & slow). Typical AVNRT: antegrade via slow pathway, retrograde via fast pathway → pseudo-R' in V1, pseudo-S in II/III/aVF. Rate 150-250, regular, narrow complex.

AVRT (30%): Accessory pathway (Bundle of Kent in WPW). Orthodromic (narrow complex, ~95%) vs Antidromic (wide complex, ~5%). WPW pattern at rest: short PR, delta wave, wide QRS.

Management Stepladder
  1. 1. Vagal manoeuvres — modified Valsalva (blow into 10ml syringe for 15s → immediately recline to supine with legs elevated 45° for 15s). Success rate ~40-50%.
  2. 2. Adenosine — 6mg rapid IV push (followed by 20ml NS flush via antecubital or above) → if no response in 1-2min → 12mg → 12mg. Warn patient: "You'll feel chest tightness and sense of doom for 10-20 seconds." CONTRAINDICATED in asthma, WPW with pre-excited AF.
  3. 3. Verapamil 2.5-5mg IV over 2min (if no HF, no beta-blocker use, not WPW) or Diltiazem.
  4. 4. If unstable → synchronized cardioversion 50-100J.

Bradyarrhythmias (HR <60 bpm)

1st Degree AV Block

PR interval >200ms (>1 large box), consistent. Every P wave conducted. Benign, no treatment needed. Common in inferior MI (AV node supplied by RCA). Monitor only.

2nd Degree — Mobitz Type I (Wenckebach)

Progressive PR prolongation until a dropped beat (P without QRS). "Grouped beating" pattern. Block at AV node level. Common in inferior MI. Usually transient, resolves in days. Treatment: Atropine 0.5mg IV if symptomatic. Pacing rarely needed.

2nd Degree — Mobitz Type II

⚠️ DANGEROUS. Fixed PR interval with intermittent dropped QRS (sudden, without progressive prolongation). Block is infra-nodal (His-Purkinje). Common in anterior MI (septal necrosis). QRS often wide. HIGH risk of progression to complete heart block. Transcutaneous pacing pads ON immediately. Transvenous pacing indicated. Atropine often INEFFECTIVE (infranodal block).

3rd Degree (Complete) Heart Block

Complete AV dissociation — P waves and QRS complexes march independently. No relationship between P and QRS. Escape rhythm: Junctional (narrow QRS, 40-60bpm, more stable) vs Ventricular (wide QRS, 20-40bpm, unreliable).

In inferior MI: Junctional escape, narrow QRS, rate 40-60, often transient (days to weeks), may respond to atropine. Often resolves.
In anterior MI: Ventricular escape, wide QRS, rate 20-40, sudden onset, very high mortality (~80%), indicates massive septal necrosis. Requires immediate transvenous pacing. Often permanent → PPM needed.

Sinus Bradycardia: Most common arrhythmia in inferior MI (due to RCA supplying SA node in 60% of people). Often a vagal response (Bezold-Jarisch reflex). Usually benign if asymptomatic & SBP adequate. Atropine 0.5mg IV if symptomatic.

Sinus Arrest / SA Block: Pause >3 seconds. If recurrent or symptomatic → Atropine, Isoproterenol infusion, transcutaneous pacing.

Bradycardia Algorithm (AHA)
  1. 1. Assess: Is the bradycardia causing symptoms? (Hypotension, altered mental status, chest pain, acute HF)
  2. 2. If symptomatic → Atropine 0.5mg IV q3-5min (max 3mg). Warning: Atropine is INEFFECTIVE for infranodal block (Mobitz II, CHB with wide QRS) and in transplanted hearts.
  3. 3. If atropine ineffective → Transcutaneous pacing (sedation + analgesia first) OR Dopamine 5-20mcg/kg/min OR Epinephrine 2-10mcg/min.
  4. 4. Transvenous pacing if: Mobitz II, CHB, symptomatic bradycardia refractory to drugs, new bifascicular block in anterior MI.

ECG Pattern Atlas — MI & Arrhythmia Recognition

Normal Sinus Rhythm (Reference)

Rate 60-100, regular. P before every QRS, QRS <120ms. PR 120-200ms. QTc <440ms (M) / <460ms (F).

STEMI — ST Elevation

Convex ST elevation ("tombstone" or "frowny face") with reciprocal ST depression in contralateral leads. Hyperacute T waves may precede ST elevation.

Ventricular Fibrillation

Chaotic, irregular, no identifiable P/QRS/T. No cardiac output. Immediate defibrillation required.

Monomorphic VT

Wide QRS (>120ms), regular, rate 150-250. Uniform morphology. AV dissociation = diagnostic. If in doubt → it's VT.

Atrial Fibrillation

Irregularly irregular. No P waves — fibrillatory baseline. Variable R-R intervals. Narrow QRS unless aberrant conduction.

Complete Heart Block (3rd Degree)

P waves and QRS march independently. P-P regular, R-R regular, but NO relationship. Wide QRS escape = ventricular origin (dangerous).

Killip Classification — Bedside Haemodynamic Assessment

Killip I — No Heart Failure

No clinical signs of HF. Clear lungs, no S3. Mortality ~6%.

Killip II — Mild HF

Bibasilar crackles (≤50% lung fields), S3 gallop, elevated JVP. Mortality ~17%.

Killip III — Pulmonary Oedema

Crackles >50% lung fields. Frank pulmonary oedema. CXR: bilateral alveolar infiltrates, Kerley B lines, pleural effusions. Mortality ~38%.

Killip IV — Cardiogenic Shock

SBP <90 for >30min, CI <2.2, PCWP >18. Cold, clammy, oliguric, confused. Mortality ~60-80%. Emergent PCI + mechanical support.

🔴 ACLS Cardiac Arrest Algorithm (VF/pVT)

1. Confirm cardiac arrest → Begin CPR (100-120/min, 2" depth, full recoil) → Attach defibrillator

2. VF/pVT identified → SHOCK (Biphasic 120-200J) → Resume CPR immediately ×2 min

3. Rhythm check → Still VF/pVT → SHOCK → Resume CPR → Epinephrine 1mg IV/IO (repeat q3-5min)

4. Rhythm check → Still VF/pVT → SHOCK → Resume CPR → Amiodarone 300mg IV bolus

5. Continue cycle. Second dose Amiodarone 150mg. Address reversible causes:

4 H's:
  • Hypovolemia
  • Hypoxia
  • Hydrogen ion (acidosis)
  • Hypo/Hyperkalemia
4 T's:
  • Tension pneumothorax
  • Tamponade (cardiac)
  • Toxins
  • Thrombosis (coronary/pulmonary)

Post-MI Discharge — "ABCDE" of Secondary Prevention

A — ACEi/ARB

Ramipril 2.5-10mg or Enalapril. Start within 24h if stable. ↓ Remodelling, ↓ mortality. ARB (Valsartan) if ACEi intolerant. Add Eplerenone (MRA) if EF ≤40% + HF/DM (EPHESUS trial).

B — Beta-Blocker

Metoprolol succinate, Bisoprolol, or Carvedilol. Proven mortality benefit (especially if EF ↓). Titrate to HR 55-65. Continue indefinitely unless contraindicated.

C — Cholesterol (Statin) + Cessation

High-intensity statin: Atorvastatin 80mg or Rosuvastatin 40mg. Target LDL <55mg/dL (ESC) or <70mg/dL. Add Ezetimibe if target not met. PCSK9i (Evolocumab) for refractory. Smoking cessation absolutely mandatory.

D — DAPT + Diabetes Control

ASA 81mg + Ticagrelor 90mg BID (or Clopidogrel 75mg) for 12 months. Consider extended DAPT (Ticagrelor 60mg BID) if high ischaemic / low bleeding risk. HbA1c <7%. SGLT2i (Empagliflozin) if HF/DM.

E — Exercise + Education

Cardiac rehabilitation (Phase I-III). 150 min/week moderate exercise. Mediterranean diet. BP <130/80. BMI <25. Annual flu vaccine. Psychosocial assessment (depression screening — common post-MI).
