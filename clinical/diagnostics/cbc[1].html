What is a CBC?

The Complete Blood Count (CBC) is the most frequently ordered laboratory test worldwide. It provides a quantitative and qualitative analysis of the three major cellular components of blood: Red Blood Cells (Erythrocytes), White Blood Cells (Leukocytes), and Platelets (Thrombocytes).

Modern automated hematology analyzers use principles of electrical impedance, light scattering, fluorescence flow cytometry, and cytochemical staining to generate CBC results. A standard CBC panel produces 15–25 measurable parameters, each with distinct clinical significance.

RBC COUNT Erythrocyte Count

Red Blood Cell Count

Total number of erythrocytes per unit volume of blood. Measured by electrical impedance (Coulter principle) or optical light scatter in automated analyzers.

Adult Male
4.5 – 5.5 × 1012/L
≈ 4.5 – 5.5 million/µL
Adult Female
3.8 – 4.8 × 1012/L
≈ 3.8 – 4.8 million/µL

🏥 Disease Diagnostic Importance

ANEMIA CLASSIFICATION

RBC count is foundational for diagnosing anemia type. Low RBC with low Hb indicates true anemia. Critical first step in workup: combined with Hct and MCV to classify as microcytic, normocytic, or macrocytic, guiding specific investigations (iron studies, B12/folate, reticulocyte count).

MYELOPROLIFERATIVE NEOPLASMS

Polycythemia Vera (PV): Markedly elevated RBC (often >6.0 × 10¹²/L) with elevated Hct (>54%), normal O₂ saturation = major diagnostic criterion. RBC count helps distinguish PV from secondary polycythemia.

CHRONIC HEMOLYSIS

Persistently low RBC with compensatory reticulocytosis is hallmark of chronic hemolytic anemia (hereditary spherocytosis, sickle cell, thalassemia major). RBC count alone cannot distinguish; combine with RPI and PBS morphology.

PROGNOSIS IN CRITICAL ILLNESS

RBC count <2.0 × 10¹²/L indicates severe anemia requiring urgent transfusion evaluation. Progressive decline predicts marrow failure (aplastic anemia, MDS) if reticulocyte response inadequate.

Clinical Significance

⬆ INCREASED (Polycythemia / Erythrocytosis)
  • • Polycythemia Vera (PV) — JAK2 V617F mutation
  • • Secondary polycythemia (chronic hypoxia, COPD, high altitude)
  • • Dehydration (relative polycythemia)
  • • EPO-secreting tumors (renal cell carcinoma, hepatoma)
  • • Smoking — chronic carbon monoxide exposure
⬇ DECREASED (Anemia)
  • • Iron deficiency anemia (most common worldwide)
  • • Megaloblastic anemia (B12/folate deficiency)
  • • Hemolytic anemias (sickle cell, thalassemia, G6PD)
  • • Aplastic anemia — bone marrow failure
  • • Chronic disease / chronic kidney disease
  • • Acute hemorrhage
📝 PEARLS

Neonates have higher RBC counts (5.0–6.5 × 1012/L) due to fetal erythropoiesis stimulated by low intrauterine pO₂. Values gradually decline to adult levels by 3–6 months of age (physiological anemia of infancy).

HEMOGLOBIN Hb / Hgb

Hemoglobin Concentration

The concentration of hemoglobin in whole blood. Measured spectrophotometrically using the cyanmethemoglobin (HiCN) method or sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) method. Hemoglobin is the primary oxygen-carrying metalloprotein — a tetramer of 2α and 2β globin chains, each bound to a heme group containing Fe²⁺.

Adult Male
13.5 – 17.5 g/dL
Adult Female
12.0 – 16.0 g/dL
WHO Anemia Cutoff
M: <13 | F: <12 g/dL

🏥 Disease Diagnostic Importance

ANEMIA SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

Hemoglobin is THE primary parameter for anemia diagnosis. Severity correlates with clinical symptoms: Hb 7–9 g/dL = fatigue/dyspnea at exertion; Hb <7 g/dL = risk of cardiac decompensation, myocardial ischemia, stroke. Directly determines transfusion thresholds in clinical practice.

HEMOGLOBINOPATHIES DIAGNOSIS

Sickle Cell Disease (HbSS): Hb typically 7–10 g/dL due to chronic hemolysis. Thalassemia Major: Hb <7 g/dL requires regular transfusions. Hemoglobin electrophoresis MUST be ordered when abnormal Hb variants detected on analyzer.

ACUTE VS CHRONIC DISEASE

Acute hemorrhage: Hb drop >1 g/dL in hours (before Hct drops) = plasma shift into interstitium. Chronic disease: gradual Hb decline allows physiological compensation. RBC count often NORMAL in chronic disease anemia despite low Hb = key differentiator.

TREATMENT MONITORING

Serial Hb tracks efficacy of iron/B12/folate replacement, EPO therapy, transfusions, and chemotherapy. Expected rise in iron deficiency = 0.2 g/dL/day. Failure to rise suggests non-compliance, continued blood loss, or alternate diagnosis.

CARDIOPULMONARY COMPENSATION

Hb <5 g/dL increases tissue hypoxia risk exponentially. Combined with Hct, predicts cardiac output demands. In perioperative settings: Hb <7 g/dL generally warrants transfusion pre-emptively in high-risk patients.

Hemoglobin Variants & Clinical Correlations

Normal Hemoglobin Types
  • • HbA (α₂β₂) — 95–97% of adult Hb
  • • HbA₂ (α₂δ₂) — 2–3.5% (↑ in β-thalassemia trait)
  • • HbF (α₂γ₂) — <1% adult; 60–80% at birth
Abnormal Hemoglobins
  • • HbS — Sickle cell (Glu→Val at β6)
  • • HbC — (Glu→Lys at β6) — target cells
  • • HbE — Common in Southeast Asia
  • • HbH — β₄ tetramers (3-gene α-thal)
⚠️ INTERFERENCE ALERT

Lipemia, hyperbilirubinemia, and very high WBC counts (>100 × 10⁹/L) can falsely elevate Hb values. Carboxyhemoglobin (HbCO) and methemoglobin (MetHb) are included in total Hb measurement but do not carry oxygen.

HEMATOCRIT Hct / PCV

Hematocrit / Packed Cell Volume

The proportion of blood volume occupied by red blood cells, expressed as a percentage or L/L. In automated analyzers, Hct is a calculated value: Hct = MCV × RBC count. In manual methods, determined by centrifugation (Wintrobe or microhematocrit method).

Adult Male
40 – 54%
Adult Female
36 – 48%

🏥 Disease Diagnostic Importance

VOLUME STATUS & HYDRATION

Hct is a calculated marker of blood composition. Elevated Hct (≥54%) with elevated RBC/Hb = true polycythemia OR hemoconcentration (dehydration). Low Hct despite normal RBC/Hb may indicate dilutional state (SIADH, heart failure). Hct >60% = hyperviscosity risk (thrombosis, stroke in PV).

ACUTE HEMORRHAGE DETECTION

Critical Finding: Early hemorrhage shows ↓RBC/Hb but NORMAL Hct initially (ratio delay). After 24–48 hrs, fluid shifts normalize Hct. Serial Hct trending (not single value) is crucial: ↓1% per 8 hrs = ongoing bleeding. Hct <30% = massive transfusion protocol likely needed.

THROMBOSIS RISK IN POLYCYTHEMIA

PV with Hct >55% increases blood viscosity → thrombotic complications (CVA, MI, DVT/PE, splanchnic vein thrombosis). Target Hct <45% in PV patients. This Hct threshold may indicate need for phlebotomy or cytoreductive therapy.

TRANSFUSION DECISION-MAKING

Modern transfusion thresholds based on Hb not Hct. However, Hct contextualizes: Hct 24% + Hb 8 g/dL = normochromic picture; Hct 20% + Hb 8 g/dL = hypochromic (suspect iron deficiency). Restrictive transfusion strategy (Hb 7–9 g/dL) reduces complications vs liberal strategy.

PRE-OPERATIVE RISK STRATIFICATION

Hct <20% pre-op = increased cardiac morbidity risk. Major surgery with Hct <25% may warrant pre-operative transfusion. Cardiac patients with Hct <25% at higher MI risk during perioperative period.

⬆ INCREASED
  • • Polycythemia vera (Hct >60% → hyperviscosity risk)
  • • Dehydration (hemoconcentration)
  • • Burns — plasma loss
  • • High altitude acclimatization
⬇ DECREASED
  • • All causes of anemia
  • • Overhydration / fluid overload
  • • Pregnancy (physiological hemodilution)
  • • Massive hemorrhage
📐 RULE OF THREE

In normocytic normochromic states: Hct ≈ 3 × Hb and RBC × 3 ≈ Hb. Deviations suggest abnormal cell size, Hb content, or analytical error. This is a useful quick quality check.

RETICULOCYTES Retic Count

Reticulocyte Count

Reticulocytes are immature RBCs containing residual RNA (ribosomal RNA). They are released from bone marrow and mature into RBCs in 1–2 days in circulation. Detected by supravital staining (New Methylene Blue / Brilliant Cresyl Blue) or fluorescent RNA dyes in automated analyzers.

Normal %
0.5 – 2.5%
Absolute Count
25–100 × 10⁹/L
Key Metric
RPI (Retic Prod Index)
📊 CORRECTED RETICULOCYTE COUNT & RPI

CRC = Retic% × (Patient Hct / Normal Hct)

RPI = CRC / Maturation Factor — RPI >3 = adequate marrow response; RPI <2 = inadequate

⬆ RETICULOCYTOSIS
  • • Hemolytic anemias (active RBC destruction)
  • • Post-hemorrhage recovery (day 3–5)
  • • Response to iron/B12/folate therapy
  • • Post-EPO administration
⬇ RETICULOCYTOPENIA
  • • Aplastic anemia
  • • Pure red cell aplasia
  • • Myelophthisic anemia
  • • Chemotherapy / radiation effect
ESR Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate

Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR)

Rate at which RBCs sediment in one hour. A non-specific marker of inflammation. Gold standard: Westergren method (200mm vertical tube, citrated blood). ESR depends on rouleaux formation driven by acute-phase proteins (fibrinogen, immunoglobulins).

Male
0 – 15 mm/hr
Age/2 (for age >50)
Female
0 – 20 mm/hr
(Age + 10)/2 (for age >50)
⬆ MARKEDLY ELEVATED (>100 mm/hr)
  • • Multiple myeloma / Waldenström's
  • • Temporal (Giant Cell) arteritis
  • • Severe infections (TB, endocarditis)
  • • Metastatic malignancy
  • • Polymyalgia rheumatica
⬇ LOW / ZERO ESR
  • • Polycythemia vera
  • • Sickle cell disease (impaired rouleaux)
  • • Severe leukocytosis
  • • Congestive heart failure
  • • Hypofibrinogenemia / DIC
TOTAL WBC Total Leukocyte Count (TLC)

White Blood Cell Count

Total number of leukocytes per unit volume. WBCs are the body's defense system — comprising granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) and agranulocytes (lymphocytes, monocytes). Measured by impedance or optical methods after RBC lysis.

Adult
4.0 – 11.0 × 10⁹/L
Neonate
10 – 26 × 10⁹/L
Child (2–6 yrs)
5 – 15 × 10⁹/L
⬆ LEUKOCYTOSIS (>11 × 10⁹/L)
  • • Bacterial infections (neutrophilic)
  • • Leukemia (CML can have WBC >100 × 10⁹/L)
  • • Physiological: exercise, stress, pregnancy
  • • Corticosteroid therapy
  • • Tissue necrosis (MI, burns)
⬇ LEUKOPENIA (<4 × 10⁹/L)
  • • Viral infections (HIV, hepatitis, EBV)
  • • Aplastic anemia
  • • Drug-induced (chemotherapy, chloramphenicol)
  • • Hypersplenism
  • • Autoimmune neutropenia / SLE
  • • Overwhelming sepsis (paradoxical)
👶 PEDIATRIC NOTE

At birth: neutrophil predominant. By day 4–7: first crossover — lymphocytes become dominant. At age 4–7 years: second crossover — neutrophils regain dominance (adult pattern). This is the famous "double crossover" of WBC differential in children.

NEUTROPHILS Polymorphonuclear Leukocytes

Neutrophils (Segmented & Band Forms)

Most abundant WBC type in adults. First responders to bacterial infection. Contain primary (azurophilic) and secondary (specific) granules. Lifespan in circulation: 6–8 hours. Half exist in the marginated pool (attached to vessel walls).

Relative
40 – 70%
Absolute (ANC)
2.0 – 7.0 × 10⁹/L
Bands (Immature)
0 – 5%
⬆ NEUTROPHILIA
  • • Bacterial infections (primary cause)
  • • Acute inflammation / tissue necrosis
  • • CML / Leukemoid reaction
  • • Corticosteroids (demargination effect)
  • • Smoking, stress, post-exercise
⬇ NEUTROPENIA
  • • Mild: 1.0–1.5 × 10⁹/L
  • • Moderate: 0.5–1.0 × 10⁹/L
  • • Severe / Agranulocytosis: <0.5 × 10⁹/L
  • • Drug-induced: anti-thyroid drugs, clozapine
  • • Viral infections, typhoid, brucellosis
🔀 LEFT SHIFT vs RIGHT SHIFT

Left Shift: Increase in immature forms (bands, metamyelocytes, myelocytes) — seen in acute bacterial infections, sepsis. Right Shift: Hypersegmented neutrophils (>5 lobes) — pathognomonic of megaloblastic anemia (B12/folate deficiency).

LYMPHOCYTES

Lymphocytes (T, B & NK Cells)

Key mediators of adaptive immunity. T-cells (60–70%) — cell-mediated immunity; B-cells (10–20%) — humoral immunity / antibody production; NK cells (10–15%) — innate immune surveillance. Small round cells with scant cytoplasm on PBS.

Relative
20 – 40%
Absolute
1.0 – 4.0 × 10⁹/L
⬆ LYMPHOCYTOSIS
  • • Viral infections (EBV — atypical lymphocytes / Downey cells)
  • • CMV, hepatitis, pertussis (whooping cough)
  • • CLL (most common adult leukemia in West)
  • • ALL (most common childhood leukemia)
  • • Toxoplasmosis
⬇ LYMPHOPENIA
  • • HIV/AIDS (CD4+ T-cell destruction)
  • • Corticosteroid therapy (lymphocyte apoptosis)
  • • Radiation / chemotherapy
  • • SLE (autoimmune destruction)
  • • Severe COVID-19 (prognostic marker)
MONO / EOS / BASO

Monocytes, Eosinophils & Basophils

Monocytes (2–8% | 0.2–0.8 × 10⁹/L)

Largest WBC. Circulate 1–3 days then migrate to tissues → become macrophages (Kupffer cells in liver, alveolar macrophages, microglia in brain, osteoclasts in bone). Key in chronic inflammation and granuloma formation.

⬆ in: TB, endocarditis, CMML, recovery from neutropenia | Mnemonic: "Monocytes for Months" (chronic infections)

Eosinophils (1–4% | 0.04–0.4 × 10⁹/L)

Bilobed nucleus, bright orange-red granules with eosin staining. Major Basic Protein (MBP) in granules is toxic to parasites. Key in allergic responses and parasitic defense.

⬆ in: NAACP — Neoplasia, Asthma/Allergy, Addison's, Collagen vascular disease, Parasites | Churg-Strauss, Löffler syndrome

Basophils (0–1% | 0–0.1 × 10⁹/L)

Rarest WBC. Large dark blue-black metachromatic granules obscure the nucleus. Contain histamine and heparin. Tissue counterpart: Mast cells. Express high-affinity IgE receptors (FcεRI).

⬆ in: CML (highly characteristic), myeloproliferative neoplasms, hypothyroidism, ulcerative colitis

🧪 HIGH-YIELD POINT

Basophilia is the most specific marker for CML among the WBC differential findings. Combined with marked leukocytosis, left shift, and eosinophilia — strongly suggests CML (confirm with BCR-ABL1 / Philadelphia chromosome).

PLATELET COUNT Thrombocyte Count

Platelet Count

Anucleate cytoplasmic fragments derived from megakaryocytes in bone marrow. Regulated by thrombopoietin (TPO) produced primarily in the liver. Each megakaryocyte produces ~1000–3000 platelets. Lifespan: 8–10 days. ~⅓ sequestered in spleen.

Normal Range
150 – 400 × 10⁹/L
Bleeding Risk
<50 × 10⁹/L
Spontaneous Bleeding
<20 × 10⁹/L
⬆ THROMBOCYTOSIS (>400 × 10⁹/L)
  • • Reactive: Iron deficiency, infection, inflammation, post-splenectomy, malignancy
  • • Primary: Essential thrombocythemia (JAK2/CALR/MPL mutations)
  • • CML, Polycythemia Vera, Myelofibrosis
  • • Post-hemorrhage rebound
⬇ THROMBOCYTOPENIA (<150 × 10⁹/L)
  • • ITP — immune destruction (anti-GPIIb/IIIa)
  • • TTP — ADAMTS13 deficiency (pentad)
  • • HUS — Shiga toxin (E.coli O157:H7)
  • • DIC — consumption coagulopathy
  • • HIT — Heparin-Induced (anti-PF4)
  • • Hypersplenism, aplastic anemia, MDS
⚠️ PSEUDOTHROMBOCYTOPENIA

EDTA-dependent platelet clumping causes falsely low counts in ~0.1% of patients. Always check PBS for clumps. Remedy: redraw in citrate tube and correct count × 1.1 (dilution factor).

MPV & PDW

Mean Platelet Volume & Platelet Distribution Width

MPV — Mean Platelet Volume

7.5 – 11.5 fL

Average size of platelets. Young platelets (reticulated) are larger and more hemostatically active. ⬆MPV = active thrombopoiesis (ITP, recovery). ⬇MPV = marrow failure, Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome (characteristically small platelets).

PDW — Platelet Distribution Width

9 – 17 fL

Measure of variation in platelet size (anisocytosis of platelets). ⬆PDW = reactive thrombocytosis, MPN, platelet activation. Used alongside MPV for differential diagnosis of thrombocytopenia causes.

💡 CLINICAL UTILITY

ITP: ⬆MPV + ⬆PDW (large young platelets replacing destroyed ones). Aplastic anemia: ⬇MPV + normal PDW (marrow failure, small uniform platelets). Bernard-Soulier Syndrome: Giant platelets → very high MPV.

MCV Mean Corpuscular Volume

MCV — Mean Corpuscular Volume

Average volume of a single RBC in femtoliters (fL). The most important index for classifying anemias morphologically. Directly measured by analyzers or calculated: MCV = Hct (%) / RBC (millions/µL) × 10

Normal Range
80 – 100 fL
Classification MCV Key Causes
Microcytic <80 fL Iron deficiency, Thalassemia, Anemia of chronic disease, Sideroblastic anemia, Lead poisoning — Mnemonic: TAILS
Normocytic 80–100 fL Acute hemorrhage, Hemolytic anemia, ACD, Aplastic anemia, CKD, Mixed deficiency
Macrocytic >100 fL Megaloblastic: B12/Folate deficiency. Non-megaloblastic: Liver disease, alcoholism, hypothyroidism, MDS, reticulocytosis
🔑 IRON DEFICIENCY vs THALASSEMIA TRAIT

Mentzer Index = MCV/RBC: >13 → Iron deficiency; <13 → Thalassemia trait. Also: RDW is ⬆ in IDA (anisocytosis) but normal in thalassemia trait (uniform microcytosis).

MCH & MCHC

MCH & MCHC — Hemoglobin Content Indices

MCH — Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin

27 – 33 pg

Average weight of Hb per RBC (in picograms). MCH = Hb (g/dL) / RBC (millions/µL) × 10. Parallels MCV — ⬇ in microcytic, ⬆ in macrocytic anemias.

MCHC — Mean Corpuscular Hb Concentration

32 – 36 g/dL

Average Hb concentration per RBC. MCHC = Hb (g/dL) / Hct (%) × 100. Most stable RBC index. Classifies as normochromic or hypochromic.

💎 HIGH-YIELD: ELEVATED MCHC (>36 g/dL)

Very few conditions raise MCHC: Hereditary Spherocytosis (most classic), cold agglutinins (artifact), severe dehydration. MCHC >36 should prompt PBS review for spherocytes and a DAT/Coombs test.

RDW Red Cell Distribution Width

RDW — Red Cell Distribution Width

Coefficient of variation of RBC volume. Quantifies anisocytosis (variation in RBC size). Generated from the RBC histogram. Higher RDW = more size variation.

11.5 – 14.5%
RDW-SD
39 – 46 fL

RDW has emerged as an independent prognostic marker for mortality in heart failure, sepsis, COVID-19, and general ICU patients — elevated RDW correlates with oxidative stress and chronic inflammation beyond hematologic disease.

🩺 Systematic Approach to CBC Interpretation

1

Check Hemoglobin → Anemia or Polycythemia?

Compare to age- and sex-specific reference ranges. WHO defines anemia as Hb <13 g/dL (M) and <12 g/dL (F).

2

Classify by MCV → Microcytic / Normocytic / Macrocytic

MCV is the single most useful index. Narrows the differential diagnosis of anemia dramatically.

3

Check RDW → Uniform or Mixed Population?

High RDW = anisocytosis. Differentiates IDA (high RDW) from thalassemia trait (normal RDW).

4

Assess Reticulocyte Count → Marrow Response?

RPI >3 = adequate response (hemolysis/hemorrhage). RPI <2 = hypoproliferative (marrow failure/deficiency).

5

Evaluate WBC & Differential → Infection / Malignancy?

Use absolute counts. Identify neutrophilia/neutropenia, lymphocytosis, eosinophilia, or left shift.

6

Check Platelet Count → Bleeding / Thrombotic Risk?

Rule out pseudothrombocytopenia. Correlate with MPV and clinical picture.

7

Review Peripheral Blood Smear

The PBS is the gold standard for morphological assessment. Look for diagnostic pointers: schistocytes, blasts, parasites, inclusions.

PANCYTOPENIA

Pancytopenia — All Lines Reduced

Simultaneous reduction in all three cell lines (RBC, WBC, Platelets). Always warrants urgent investigation including bone marrow examination.

Hypocellular Marrow
  • • Aplastic anemia (idiopathic, drug-induced)
  • • Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH)
  • • Fanconi anemia (constitutional)
Cellular / Infiltrated Marrow
  • • Megaloblastic anemia (ineffective hematopoiesis)
  • • MDS, Acute leukemia, Hairy cell leukemia
  • • Myelofibrosis, Metastatic infiltration
  • • Hypersplenism, SLE, HIV, Leishmaniasis

🔬 Pre-Analytical Variables & Quality Control

Sample Collection Errors

  • • Under-filling EDTA tube: excess EDTA → RBC crenation, ⬇MCV, ⬇Hct
  • • Clotted sample: falsely low counts for all cell lines
  • • Hemolyzed sample: ⬇RBC, ⬆K⁺, ⬆LDH, falsely ⬆Hb
  • • Delayed processing (>6hrs): WBC degranulation, ⬆MCV (cell swelling)

Instrument Flags & Interference

  • • Cold agglutinins: falsely ⬇RBC, ⬆MCV, ⬆MCHC (warm sample to 37°C)
  • • Giant platelets: counted as RBCs → falsely ⬆RBC
  • • Nucleated RBCs: counted as WBCs → falsely ⬆WBC (need correction)
  • • Platelet clumps (EDTA): pseudothrombocytopenia → redraw in citrate

CBC Reference Guide — For Medical Education & Clinical Practice

Always correlate laboratory findings with clinical context. Values may vary by laboratory and methodology.
